## 1. What the tophet is

"Tophet" is a modern scholarly label. Archaeologists borrowed it from the Hebrew Bible, where Topheth is a place near Jerusalem condemned for burning sons and daughters (S16; S7). The Carthaginians' own name for the site is not established in the sources consulted, so this remains a research gap. Scholars also call it the *Precinct of Tanit* (S7) or the *Sanctuary of Ba'l Ḥammon* (S18), after the two deities named on its inscriptions.

At Carthage it is an open-air precinct close to the ancient harbours, about 50 m west of the rectangular "commercial" harbour (S7). Over several centuries people dug pits there and buried ceramic urns containing **cremated remains of very young humans and of young animals**, mostly lambs and some kids, birds and occasionally fish. Many burials were marked with stone cippi and stelae (S7; S1; S9). Comparable precincts exist at other Phoenician and Punic sites in **North Africa, Sicily (Motya) and Sardinia (e.g. Tharros, Sulcis, Monte Sirai)**. No convincing example is known from the Phoenician homeland in the Levant (S10; S7 fig. 1).

## 2. Discovery and excavation

- **December 1921:** Paul Gielly, a public official interested in antiquities, saw a local antiquities trafficker removing stelae by moonlight. He told François Icard, chief of police in Tunis. The two bought the plot (c. 1,060 m²) and began digging, soon under the oversight of the Antiquities Service (S7).
- **1924–1925:** Count Byron Khun de Prorok dug with the epigraphist Abbé Chabot. In 1925 a Franco-American expedition under Francis W. Kelsey (University of Michigan) took over. It removed about 1,000 urns in a single three-month season, with little stratigraphic control (S7).
- **1934–36 and 1940s:** G.G. Lapeyre, then Pierre Cintas (S7).
- **1976–1979:** The American Schools of Oriental Research (ASOR) Punic Project, directed by Lawrence Stager, excavated within UNESCO's international campaign to save Carthage. The team recorded urns stratigraphically in a trench of about 150 m², and these urns are the basis of all modern bone studies (S7; S1; S2).
- **2014–2018:** New Tunisian-led excavations (Ben Jerbania et al.; S18, known only through S9). The reviewer of the 2023 volume calls them the most robust archaeological data on the tophet.

Excavator Donald Harden divided the precinct's use into three phases: Tanit I (c. 800–600 BC), Tanit II (c. 600–300 BC) and Tanit III (c. 300–146 BC). Stager's team refined this into eight phases (S7). Use ended with Rome's destruction of Carthage in 146 BC.

## 3. The physical evidence

**Urns and their contents.** All urns from the American excavations contained burnt bone (S7 fig. 18). Analysts found human infants alone, animals alone, and **infants and lambs together in the same urn** (S7; S1). Stager and later Fulton et al. report that humans and animals received the same treatment: cremation on an open-air pyre, then burial of the remains in an urn in a pit (S7; S19 via S9). Stager reads the lambs' ages as evidence of spring offerings (S7). That reading is an interpretation (C27, C31).

**Amulets.** A minority of urns, about a quarter by Stager's count, contained beads and amulets placed *after* cremation. They occur only with human remains (S7; S20 via S9). The percentage depends on which urns are counted (S9).

**Stelae and inscriptions.** Thousands of stone markers survive. Many inscriptions record a **vow (ndr)** to *Tanit, Face of Ba'l* and to *Ba'l Ḥammon*. They typically end with a formula meaning that the god heard the dedicant's voice and blessed him, showing the offering followed a granted request (S7 fig. 16). Some stelae use the term **mlk**, including the compound *mlk ʾmr*. Since Eissfeldt (1935), most scholars read *mlk* as a type of offering rather than the name of a god "Moloch". Stager reads *mlk ʾmr* as "offering of a lamb" (S7). The exact meaning of these terms is an interpretation (C13).

**Iconography.** A 4th-century BC stela found in the 1920s shows a priest holding an infant. Stager reads this as preparation for sacrifice. Schwartz et al. read the child as already dead (S7). The image fits both readings and cannot settle the question.

## 4. The written sources

- **Diodorus Siculus** (1st c. BC) wrote that during Agathocles' siege of 310 BC, the Carthaginians blamed their misfortunes on having substituted bought children for their own. He says they then sacrificed 200 children of the noblest families to Cronus, using a bronze statue with outstretched hands over a fiery pit (S12). He was writing about 250 years after the event.
- **Plutarch** (1st–2nd c. AD) describes parents offering their own children, people buying children from the poor, mothers forbidden to weep, and music drowning out the cries (S13). The passage serves his moral argument against superstition.
- Schwartz et al. note that the sacrifice tradition rests on Diodorus and the earlier Kleitarchos (3rd c. BC) as supposed eyewitness reports, together with Old Testament references (S1). The Kleitarchos text was not consulted.
- **The authors were all outsiders.** They were Greek or Roman writers, and some wrote after Rome had destroyed Carthage. The Punic sources that survive are inscriptions, not narratives. Both sides of the debate agree that the classical texts are hostile and must be read critically. They disagree on whether hostility means invention (S7; S15; S5 via S11).

## 5. The two interpretations

### A. Infant sacrifice (Stager, Smith, Greene, Xella, Quinn and others)
1. **Age profile.** Smith et al. aged the cremated infants and found most died at **about 1–1.5 months**. They argue this differs markedly from the age profile typical of infant cemeteries, and that sacrifice is therefore very probable (S2; S4, as quoted in S7).
2. **Vows.** The inscriptions describe offerings promised and then given after a god granted a request. Stager argues it makes little sense to offer an already-dead stillborn in fulfilment of a vow (S7).
3. **Animal substitution.** Lambs were treated exactly like infants and sometimes burned with them, which suggests both were offerings (S7; S19 via S9).
4. **Texts.** Greek, Roman and biblical texts independently describe child sacrifice among Phoenicians and Canaanites (S5 via S11; S7).
5. **Separate precinct.** Infants are rare in Carthage's ordinary cemeteries, and the tophet treated them in a distinct ritual way (S7).

### B. Cemetery for the very young, with sacrifice not proven (Schwartz, Houghton, Bondioli, Macchiarelli; Bénichou-Safar; Fantar; Moscati after 1987)
1. **Age profile.** Analysing 348 urns from the same excavations (540 individuals), Schwartz et al. found a large share of prenatal and perinatal individuals. They concluded the profile is consistent with natural perinatal mortality, which would be high in an ancient city (S1).
2. **Teeth.** A neonatal line in tooth enamel forms only if a baby survives birth by some days. They found it in 24 of 50 sampled individuals (S1).
3. **Conclusion.** They see tophets as cemeteries for those who died shortly before or after birth, whatever the cause. They do not exclude occasional sacrifice (S1), and call the case for sacrifice "at best, not proven" (S3).
4. **Hostile sources.** The famous accounts come from Carthage's enemies or from much later writers. Fantar argues the burials would probably not be read as sacrifice without them (S15).

### Why the same bones gave different answers
Both teams studied **the same ASOR collection** (S4). They disagree on method (S1; S6; S7):
- **Heat shrinkage.** Cremation shrinks bone and teeth. Smith et al. say Schwartz under-corrected for this and so underestimated ages. Schwartz et al. tested size increases of up to 25% and say some individuals still classify as prenatal (S1).
- **Neonatal line.** Smith et al. argue cremation can obliterate the line, so its absence does not prove death before or at birth. Schwartz et al. dispute this (S6; S7).
- **Which elements were measured.** The teams used different bones and teeth and different reference standards.

The exchange ran in *Antiquity* from 2011 to 2017 and was not resolved (S2–S6).

## 6. Where the debate stands now (2023–2024)

- A 2023 special issue edited by Garnand and Greene, *Infants as votive offerings* (*Journal of Ancient History* 11.2), broadly argues for sacrifice. Its independent reviewer (McCarty, BMCR 2024) concludes it **does not offer firm answers**. He judges the archaeology of the Carthage tophet as uncertain as ever, and notes that urn counts disagree between publications (S9).
- The final report of the 1976–79 American excavations is still unpublished (S7 lists it as forthcoming; S9).
- At **Zita** (Tunisia), a tophet used *after* Carthage's fall, an open-access 2024 study of 12 cremated children found signs of hard lives and possible systemic health problems. Their burial was careful, and the authors found no concrete indication of sacrifice. They state they cannot say whether the precinct was a cemetery, a sacrificial area or both (S8).
- Xella (2023) argues the tophet was an old Levantine tradition, probably from Tyre, rather than a colonial invention, despite the lack of eastern examples (S10).

## 7. What the evidence can and cannot establish

**Can establish:**
- An open-air precinct at Carthage received cremated infants and young animals over several centuries.
- Many burials were marked by stelae recording vows to Ba'l Ḥammon and Tanit.
- Humans and animals were sometimes cremated together.
- The site was distinct from ordinary cemeteries.
- Ancient Greek and Roman writers accused Carthage of child sacrifice.
- Expert teams analysing the same remains reached opposite conclusions.

**Cannot establish:**
- How each infant died. Cremated remains show no cause of death.
- Whether all, some or none were sacrificed.
- How many people were buried in total. The figure of 20,000 urns is a 1984 extrapolation for 400–200 BC (S14), not a count.
- Whether Diodorus's specific account of 200 children in 310 BC is accurate.
- What ordinary Carthaginians believed the rite meant.

## 8. Genuinely unresolved questions
1. Were the infants killed, or did they die naturally and receive a special rite, or both at different times?
2. If sacrifice occurred, was it routine (seasonal vows) or exceptional (crisis)?
3. Why do the two osteological age profiles diverge? Could an independent blind re-analysis or a new method settle it?
4. Did the practice come from the Phoenician homeland or develop in the western colonies?
5. How much of the classical tradition reflects real practice, and how much is enemy propaganda?

## 9. Sensitivity notes
- **The subject is the death of infants.** Avoid graphic description, avoid reconstructions of killing, and never use images that depict harm to children.
- **The debate has a modern political dimension.** Stager links "revisionism" to postcolonial scholarship (S7). Xella et al. argue that Phoenicians were long portrayed by outsiders (S5). Tunisian scholars (Fantar, Ben Jerbania and others) are central voices and must be represented, not just quoted through Western authors.

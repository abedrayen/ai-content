## Proposed title
**Primary:** *The Children of Carthage: Same Bones, Two Verdicts*

Alternatives:
- *Sacrifice or Cemetery? The Mystery of the Carthage Tophet*
- *What the Bones of Carthage Can't Tell Us*

Avoid "Baby Sacrifice Revealed", "The Dark Secret of Carthage" and anything that presents a verdict. The title must not promise an answer the evidence can't give.

## Central question
> When two expert teams studied the same cremated remains from ancient Carthage, why did they reach opposite conclusions about how these children died, and what would it take to know?

This framing makes the **method of knowing** the mystery, rather than the horror. That suits the brand's "mystery without misinformation" principle and is fair to both sides.

## Audience hook (first 10–15 seconds)
> December 1921. On the edge of Tunis, a man is caught digging up carved stones by moonlight. Beneath them lie thousands of small clay urns, and inside them, the burnt bones of babies and lambs. For a century, experts have asked one question: were these children sacrificed, or mourned?

Every element is sourced: C04, C08, C09 and C32. "Thousands" across the whole precinct is safe: about 1,000 urns were lifted in 1925 alone (C05), and later campaigns recovered more. Don't put a precise number on it.

## Recommended format and duration
- **Primary: long-form, about 10–11 minutes.** The story needs room for context, the evidence, a fair hearing of both sides, and method. A shorter piece would push us toward a verdict.
- **Companion short (45–55 s): "Two teams, same bones, opposite answers."** A single focused idea (C21, C24, C25), ending on the open question. It is not a cut-down of the long-form.
- **Hold back a second short** on the 1921 discovery (C04, C05) as a standalone story about early archaeology.

## Main factual claims we can state plainly
- Discovery story and excavation history (C04, C05)
- The urns hold cremated infants and young animals, sometimes together (C08, C09)
- Vow inscriptions to Ba'l Ḥammon and Tanit (C12)
- Amulets occur only with human remains (C14)
- Diodorus and Plutarch wrote these accounts, and they were outsiders writing long after the events (C16–C18), stated as facts *about the texts*
- Two teams studied the same remains and disagree (C21), and the dispute is about method (C24)
- The latest review calls the evidence uncertain (C29)

## Claims that must be avoided or carefully qualified
1. **Any verdict.** Neither "they sacrificed children" nor "it was just a cemetery" (C32).
2. **20,000:** an extrapolation of urns, not of children and not of sacrifices. Recommend omitting it (C11).
3. **The bronze statue and the fire-pit:** only as Diodorus's account, clearly attributed and dated (C16).
4. **"Moloch":** don't present it as Carthage's god (C13).
5. **Exact urn totals, animal counts and age percentages:** the published figures conflict. Attribute any figure to its study, or keep it qualitative (C09, C10, C22, C23).
6. **The Zita findings must not be presented as evidence about Carthage** (C30).
7. **"Roman propaganda invented it":** the hostility of the sources does not prove invention (C18).
8. **The founding year:** say "traditionally 9th century BC" (C01).

## Suggested ending
> A hundred years after that night in 1921, the urns have been counted, the bones measured, the inscriptions read. And still, two teams of experts look at the same tiny fragments and see two different stories: children given to the gods, or children given back to them. Perhaps the most honest thing archaeology can say is the hardest to hear. We don't know yet. But the answer is still there, in the ground beneath Carthage.

*(The phrase "given back to them" is an interpretive flourish. Editorial review should decide whether it suggests a meaning not supported by C22. A neutral alternative: "children offered to the gods, or children mourned and buried with care.")*

## Perspective and voices
- Give due weight to **Tunisian scholarship and institutions**: M'hamed Hassine Fantar, the Institut National du Patrimoine, and the 2014–18 Tunisian excavations (S15, S18). This is a North African heritage story, not only a Western academic debate. It fits the brand's commitment to underrepresented perspectives.
- If possible, invite a short comment from a specialist on each side, or at least name and attribute each position clearly.

## Risks
- **Platform policy:** content about harm to children may be age-restricted or demonetised. Keep the language clinical, use no shocking thumbnail, and title the short around the *scientific mystery*.
- **Accuracy risk:** several key papers were read only in abstract or extract. **Before the script is locked, the full texts of S2, S4, S5 and S6 and the Garnand et al. 2013 chart should be read** (see the research gaps in `research.md`).
- **Recommend specialist review:** ask a Punic epigrapher or bioarchaeologist to read the script before recording.

## Target
- About 1,300 words, aimed at **8:40–9:10** of speech. The current storyboard renders **8:49**. Scene timings will be refitted to your read.
- Pace: about 2.4 words per second, slower than the Short. Leave space after each section (`///`).
- `/` short breath · `//` full pause (about 0.7 s) · `///` section break (about 1.5 s) · **bold** = light emphasis.

## Delivery notes by section
1. **Cold open:** quiet and present-tense, like telling a story at night. Don't rush "urn after urn".
2. **Carthage:** warmer and more expansive. "Tradition names its founder…" must sound like tradition, not fact.
3. **The discovery:** a factual, clear rhythm. Read the numbers plainly.
4. **The accusation:** neutral reporting voice. Say "he claimed" with weight. No horror in "two hundred children". "Hostile sources are not necessarily false. // But on their own, / they are not proof." is a key line, so slow down.
5. **Voices in stone:** a softer tone. Read the vow formula ("heard his voice and blessed him") as a quotation, gently.
6. **The science:** a teacher's clarity. Pause before "But fire shrinks bone."
7. **Two verdicts:** **match the two halves exactly**: the same pace, pitch and energy for Schwartz's team and for Smith's team. Treat "Same bones. // Two verdicts." as a pair of equal weight.
8. **Beyond Carthage:** a lighter tone, opening outwards.
9–10. **Close:** slow and honest. "We don't know yet." is calm, not dramatic. Let the last line breathe.

## Narration with pacing marks
1 · Cold open
> December 1921. // On the edge of Tunis, / a local official named Paul Gielly notices a man on his hands and knees in the moonlight, / prising carved stones out of the ground. //
> What lay beneath those stones would become one of the most argued-over sites in archaeology: / urn after urn of burnt bones. // The bones of babies, / and of young lambs. //
> Two teams of scientists have since studied the same remains. // They reached **conflicting** conclusions. //
> Were these children offered to the gods? // Or mourned, / and given a burial of their own? ///

2 · Carthage
> Carthage stood on the Gulf of Tunis, / in what is now Tunisia. // It was a Phoenician foundation, / linked to the great port of Tyre, / some two thousand three hundred kilometres to the east. // Tradition names its founder as a princess of Tyre: / Elissa, / better known as Dido. //
> From the sixth century BC it grew into a trading power whose reach spanned much of the Mediterranean, / until Rome destroyed it in 146 BC. //
> Close to its ancient harbours lies an open-air sacred precinct. // Archaeologists call it the **tophet**. // It is the same word the Hebrew Bible uses for a condemned place of child burning near Jerusalem. ///

3 · The discovery
> The first excavators moved fast. // In a single season in 1925, / an expedition led by Francis Kelsey lifted out around a thousand urns, / recording little about where each one lay. //
> It took until the 1970s, / and an international campaign to save ancient Carthage, / for a team led by the American archaeologist Lawrence Stager to dig the precinct layer by layer. //
> In a trench of roughly 150 square metres they recovered hundreds of urns, / buried over some six centuries, / from around the eighth century BC until the city fell. //
> Inside were the cremated remains of very young infants. // Some urns held only animals: / mostly young lambs, / a few birds. // Some held an infant and a lamb **together**. //
> And in about three in ten of the urns studied, / someone had placed small amulets. // They were only ever with human remains, / and only after the fire. ///

4 · The accusation
> Long before any excavation, / Carthage already had a reputation. //
> Writing in the first century BC, / the Greek historian Diodorus Siculus described a siege in 310 BC. // He **claimed** that the Carthaginians, / fearing their gods had turned against them, / sacrificed two hundred children of their noblest families / to a god he called Cronus. //
> Later, / the Greek writer Plutarch wrote of parents offering their own children, / and of mothers forbidden to weep. //
> But these are accusations by outsiders: / Greek authors, / not Carthaginians. // And Diodorus wrote more than two centuries after the siege he describes. //
> Jeffrey Schwartz's team points out that the sacrifice interpretation rests partly on accounts like these, / which they call "supposed eye-witness reports". //
> Hostile sources are not necessarily false. // But on their own, / they are not proof. ///

5 · Voices in stone
> The Carthaginians themselves left something different: / stone. // Above many urns stand carved markers. // Their inscriptions, / in the Punic language, / record a vow to the goddess Tanit and the god Ba'al Hammon, / and often a reason: / because the god "heard his voice, / and blessed him". //
> For Lawrence Stager, / that wording was crucial. // These were promised gifts, / given after a prayer was answered. // That, he argued, / is hard to reconcile with an infant who had already died. //
> A few inscriptions use a word, / molk. // Following the scholar Otto Eissfeldt, / many researchers, / including Paolo Xella, / read it as the name of a kind of sacrificial offering, / not a god called "Moloch". //
> Even the carvings divide experts. // One stela shows a priest holding an infant. // Stager saw a living child, / about to be offered. // Schwartz's team see an infant who has already died. ///

6 · The scientific investigation
> Which brings us to the bones themselves. //
> Cremated infant bones are tiny, / fragile, / and distorted by fire. // To estimate age at death, / specialists measure fragments of the skull and hip, / and examine developing teeth. // The case for or against sacrifice depends largely on those ages. //
> One clue is a faint line in the tooth enamel, / called the neonatal line. // It forms only if a baby survives at least a week or two after birth. //
> But fire shrinks bone. // It may damage the enamel too. // How much you correct for that / changes the answer. ///

7 · Two verdicts
> Jeffrey Schwartz and his colleagues studied cremated remains from 348 urns from the 1970s excavation. // In their 2017 summary, / about a quarter of the individuals were prenatal, / and many more died around birth / or within the first month. // They found the neonatal line in 24 of the 50 individuals they sampled. //
> To them, / that pattern matches natural infant mortality in an ancient city. // The tophet, / they argue, / was a burial place for those who died shortly before or after birth, / whatever the cause, / though they accept the Carthaginians may occasionally have sacrificed humans. //
> Patricia Smith's team examined largely the same collection. // Correcting for shrinkage, / and arguing that cremation can erase the neonatal line, / they concluded that most of the infants died at one to one and a half months: / past birth, / and in their view unlike an ordinary infant cemetery. // To them, / sacrifice was very probable. //
> Same bones. // Two verdicts. // In exchanges running from 2010 to 2017, / each team argued in print that the other had made mistakes. ///

8 · Beyond Carthage
> Carthage was not alone. // Precincts like this appear across the central Mediterranean, / in North Africa, / Sardinia, / Sicily, / and perhaps Malta. // Yet no convincing example has been found in the Phoenician homeland itself. //
> Paolo Xella argues the custom still came from the east, / probably from Tyre. // The question remains open. //
> At Zita, / in southern Tunisia, / a tophet used after Carthage fell, / researchers studying twelve cremated children found signs of hard lives and illness, / and of careful burial. // They found no concrete sign of sacrifice, / but could not say whether the precinct was a cemetery, / a place of sacrifice, / or both. //
> And at Carthage, / Tunisian-led excavations since 2014 are dating the precinct's layers more precisely than ever before. ///

9 · What we can and cannot know
> So what can the evidence establish? // That for some six centuries, / people here cremated infants and young animals, / and buried them with care, / under stones carved with vows. // That their enemies accused them of child sacrifice. //
> What it cannot yet establish / is how each of these children died. // In 2024, / one independent review of the newest research concluded that the interpretation of the tophet remains as uncertain as ever. ///

10 · Closing
> A hundred years after that night in 1921, / the urns have been excavated, / the bones measured, / the inscriptions read. // And still, / the evidence leaves two stories standing: / children offered to the gods, / or children mourned, / and buried with care. //
> Perhaps the most honest thing archaeology can say / is the hardest to hear. // We don't know yet. // But the answer may still be there, / in the ground beneath Carthage.

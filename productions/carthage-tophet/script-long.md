## 1 · Cold open (0:00–0:42)
**[FOUND]** December 1921. On the edge of Tunis, a local official named Paul Gielly notices a man on his hands and knees in the moonlight, prising carved stones out of the ground. *(c04)*
What lay beneath those stones would become one of the most argued-over sites in archaeology: urn after urn of burnt bones. The bones of babies, and of young lambs. *(c08)*
Two teams of scientists have since studied the same remains. They reached conflicting conclusions. *(c21)*
**[UNKNOWN]** Were these children offered to the gods? Or mourned, and given a burial of their own? *(c32)*

## 2 · Carthage (0:42–1:38)
**[FOUND]** Carthage was a Phoenician city, linked to the great port of Tyre, some 2,300 kilometres to the east. Tradition names its founder as a princess of Tyre: Elissa, better known as Dido. *(c35, c41)* It stood on the Gulf of Tunis, in what is now Tunisia. *(c01)*
*(2026-11-17: sentence order changed to match the map sequence, from Tyre to Carthage and then the Gulf of Tunis. The voiced text is in `narration-long.json`.)*
From the 6th century BC it grew into a trading power whose reach spanned much of the Mediterranean, until Rome destroyed it in 146 BC. *(c01)*
Close to its ancient harbours lies an open-air sacred precinct. *(c06)* Archaeologists call it the tophet. It is the same word the Hebrew Bible uses for a condemned place of child burning near Jerusalem. *(c03)*

## 3 · The discovery (1:38–2:58)
**[FOUND]** The first excavators moved fast. In a single season in 1925, an expedition led by Francis Kelsey lifted out around a thousand urns, recording little about where each one lay. *(c05)*
It took until the 1970s, and an international campaign to save ancient Carthage, for a team led by the American archaeologist Lawrence Stager to dig the precinct layer by layer. *(c05)*
In a trench of roughly 150 square metres they recovered hundreds of urns, buried over some six centuries, from around the 8th century BC until the city fell. *(c10, c07)*
Inside were the cremated remains of very young infants. Some urns held only animals: mostly young lambs, a few birds. Some held an infant and a lamb together. *(c08, c09)*
And in about three in ten of the urns studied, someone had placed small amulets. They were only ever with human remains, and only after the fire. *(c14)*

## 4 · The accusation (2:58–4:00)
**[TEXT]** Long before any excavation, Carthage already had a reputation.
Writing in the 1st century BC, the Greek historian Diodorus Siculus described a siege in 310 BC. He claimed that the Carthaginians, fearing their gods had turned against them, sacrificed two hundred children of their noblest families to a god he called Cronus. *(c16)*
Later, the Greek writer Plutarch wrote of parents offering their own children, and of mothers forbidden to weep. *(c17)*
But these are accusations by outsiders: Greek authors, not Carthaginians. And Diodorus wrote more than two centuries after the siege he describes. *(c18, c16)*
**[INTERPRETATION]** Jeffrey Schwartz's team points out that the sacrifice interpretation rests partly on accounts like these, which they call "supposed eye-witness reports". *(c39, Schwartz et al. 2010)*
Hostile sources are not necessarily false. But on their own, they are not proof.

> *Visuals: source cards in parchment tone. The author's name, work and date, plus a timeline bar showing the gap between 310 BC and the 1st century BC. No illustration of the scenes they describe.*

## 5 · Voices in stone (4:00–5:08)
**[FOUND]** The Carthaginians themselves left something different: stone. Above many urns stand carved markers. Their inscriptions, in the Punic language, record a vow to the goddess Tanit and the god Ba'al Hammon, and often a reason: because the god "heard his voice and blessed him". *(c12)*
**[INTERPRETATION]** For Lawrence Stager, that wording was crucial. These were promised gifts, given after a prayer was answered. That, he argued, is hard to reconcile with an infant who had already died. *(c26, Stager 2014)*
**[INTERPRETATION]** A few inscriptions use a word, *mlk*. Following the scholar Otto Eissfeldt, many researchers, including Paolo Xella, read it as the name of a kind of sacrificial offering, not a god called "Moloch". *(c13, Eissfeldt 1935; Xella 2023)*
**[INTERPRETATION]** Even the carvings divide experts. One stela shows a priest holding an infant. Stager saw a living child, about to be offered. Schwartz's team see an infant who has already died. *(c15)*

> *Visuals: the stela, shown whole, with the inscription formula animated as text beside it (translation credited to Stager 2014). The letters "mlk" set in type. Two attribution chips for the priest-stela reading. The priest stela itself is not shown: no licensed image.*

## 6 · The scientific investigation (5:08–6:12)
**[FOUND]** Which brings us to the bones themselves.
Cremated infant bones are tiny, fragile and distorted by fire. To estimate age at death, specialists measure fragments of the skull and hip, and examine developing teeth. *(c24, c40)*
The case for or against sacrifice depends largely on those ages. *(c40)*
One clue is a faint line in the tooth enamel, called the neonatal line. It forms only if a baby survives at least a week or two after birth. *(c25)*
But fire shrinks bone. It may damage the enamel too. How much you correct for that changes the answer. *(c24)*

> *Visuals: animated schematic of a developing tooth crown, with enamel layers growing and the neonatal line appearing at birth. Labelled "Simplified diagram · not to scale". A shrinkage animation: an outline contracting by up to 25%, the range Schwartz et al. tested.*

## 7 · Two verdicts (6:12–7:44)
**[INTERPRETATION]** Jeffrey Schwartz and his colleagues studied cremated remains from 348 urns from the 1970s excavation. *(c22)* In their 2017 summary, about a quarter of the individuals were prenatal, and many more died around birth or within the first month. *(c38, Schwartz et al. 2017)* They found the neonatal line in 24 of the 50 individuals they sampled. *(c25)*
To them, that pattern matches natural infant mortality in an ancient city. The tophet, they argue, was a burial place for those who died shortly before or after birth, whatever the cause, though they accept the Carthaginians may occasionally have sacrificed humans. *(c22, Schwartz et al. 2010, 2012, 2017)*
**[INTERPRETATION]** Patricia Smith's team examined largely the same collection. *(c21)* Correcting for shrinkage, and arguing that cremation can erase the neonatal line, they concluded that most of the infants died at one to one and a half months: past birth, and in their view unlike an ordinary infant cemetery. To them, sacrifice was very probable. *(c23, c24, Smith et al. 2011, 2013)*
**[FOUND]** Same bones. Two verdicts. In exchanges running from 2010 to 2017, each team argued in print that the other had made mistakes. *(c21, c24)*

## 8 · Beyond Carthage (7:44–8:34)
**[FOUND]** Carthage was not alone. Precincts like this appear across the central Mediterranean, in North Africa, Sardinia, Sicily and perhaps Malta. Yet no convincing example has been found in the Phoenician homeland itself. *(c19)*
**[INTERPRETATION]** Paolo Xella argues the custom still came from the east, probably from Tyre. The question remains open. *(c20)*
**[FOUND]** At Zita, in southern Tunisia, a tophet used after Carthage fell, researchers studying twelve cremated children found signs of hard lives and illness, and of careful burial. They found no concrete sign of sacrifice, but could not say whether the precinct was a cemetery, a place of sacrifice, or both. *(c30)*
And at Carthage, Tunisian-led excavations since 2014 are dating the precinct's layers more precisely than ever before. *(c37)*

> *Visuals: the map returns. Markers for Carthage, Motya, Tharros and Zita light up in sequence. The Levant shows a dashed question-mark ring around Tyre. A Zita callout card. Note on screen: "comparative evidence · different site and period".*

## 9 · What we can and cannot know (8:34–9:20)
**[FOUND]** So what can the evidence establish? That for some six centuries, people here cremated infants and young animals, and buried them with care, under stones carved with vows. *(c07, c08, c12, c14)* That their enemies accused them of child sacrifice. *(c16, c18)*
**[UNKNOWN]** What it cannot yet establish is how each of these children died. *(c32, c40)* In 2024, one independent review of the newest research concluded that the interpretation of the tophet remains as uncertain as ever. *(c29)*

> *Visuals: two columns, "The evidence shows" and "The evidence cannot yet show", built up line by line.*

## 10 · Closing (9:20–9:50)
**[UNKNOWN]** A hundred years after that night in 1921, the urns have been excavated, the bones measured, the inscriptions read. And still, the evidence leaves two stories standing: children offered to the gods, or children mourned, and buried with care. *(c32)*
Perhaps the most honest thing archaeology can say is the hardest to hear. We don't know yet. But the answer may still be there, in the ground beneath Carthage. *(c37)*
